01 / Definition

What Do We Mean by "Aliens"?

When most people think of aliens, they imagine little green men in flying saucers. But scientists use the word "alien" to mean any life form that did not originate on Earth. That could mean bacteria living deep in the rocks of Mars, or it could mean an advanced civilisation capable of building spacecraft and communicating across the stars. The word "extraterrestrial" — meaning "outside of Earth" — is often used in science. Both words refer to the same idea: life beyond our planet.

Scientists generally divide the search into two types. The first is the search for simple life, such as microbes — tiny single-celled organisms similar to bacteria on Earth. The second is the search for intelligent life, meaning beings that can think, communicate, and use technology. So far, we have not found clear evidence of either kind. But the search continues.

"Extraterrestrial" comes from the Latin extra (outside) and terra (Earth). The search for such life is one of the greatest scientific quests of our time.

02 / Scale

The Size of the Universe

To understand why scientists believe aliens might exist, we first need to think about the enormous size of the universe. Our planet Earth is part of the solar system, which includes the Sun and eight planets. Our solar system is part of the Milky Way galaxy, which contains over 200 billion stars. And the Milky Way is just one of an estimated two trillion galaxies in the observable universe.

That number — two trillion galaxies — is almost impossible to imagine. If each galaxy has hundreds of billions of stars, and many of those stars have planets, the total number of planets in the universe is unimaginably large. Scientists estimate there could be more planets than there are grains of sand on all of Earth's beaches combined.

With so many worlds out there, many scientists find it hard to believe that life exists only here, on this one small planet, in one ordinary solar system, in one of trillions of galaxies.

03 / Biology

The Conditions for Life

On Earth, life needs a few key things to survive: liquid water, a source of energy, and the right chemical building blocks — especially carbon. Life has been found in some of the most extreme environments on our planet: boiling hot springs, frozen glaciers, and even deep in the ocean where no sunlight reaches. These tough creatures, called extremophiles, show us that life can adapt to surprisingly harsh conditions.

Scientists look for similar conditions on other planets and moons. A planet is said to be in the "habitable zone" — sometimes called the Goldilocks Zone — if it is not too hot and not too cold for liquid water to exist on its surface. Many such planets have been discovered. But even outside this zone, life might exist in unexpected places.

The "Goldilocks Zone" is named after the fairy tale: not too hot, not too cold — just right for liquid water to exist, and possibly for life to begin.

04 / Nearby

Life in Our Solar System

Our own solar system may be hiding signs of life closer than we think. Mars, our neighbouring planet, once had liquid water flowing across its surface billions of years ago. Today, Mars is cold and dry, but scientists have detected signs of water ice underground. Some researchers believe that if life existed on ancient Mars, it may have survived deep below the surface, where liquid water might still exist.

Even more exciting are some of the moons in our solar system. Europa, a moon of Jupiter, has a vast ocean of liquid water beneath its icy surface. Heat from the moon's interior keeps this ocean from freezing. Scientists believe this hidden ocean could support microbial life. Another moon, Enceladus, orbiting Saturn, actually sprays jets of water into space — and those jets contain organic chemicals, the building blocks of life.

05 / Signals

Searching for Alien Signals

If intelligent life exists elsewhere in the universe, it might be trying to communicate with other civilisations — just as we have done. Since 1960, scientists have been listening to the sky for radio signals that might come from an alien civilisation. This effort is known as SETI, the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence.

So far, no confirmed signal has been found. But in 1977, a radio telescope in Ohio detected an unusual signal that seemed very different from natural sources. A scientist reviewing the data wrote "Wow!" in the margin — and the "Wow! Signal" has never been explained or heard again.

Humans have also sent messages into space. In 1974, scientists used the Arecibo radio telescope in Puerto Rico to send a message toward a cluster of stars 25,000 light-years away. We have also launched two Voyager spacecraft, each carrying a "Golden Record" — a metal disc containing music, greetings in 55 languages, and images of life on Earth. These probes are now beyond our solar system, drifting through interstellar space.

06 / Paradox

The Fermi Paradox

Given the enormous size of the universe and the huge number of planets that might support life, a simple question arises: if alien life is so likely, where is everyone? This is known as the Fermi Paradox, named after the physicist Enrico Fermi, who asked this question in 1950.

Many explanations have been proposed. Perhaps intelligent life is extremely rare — so rare that we are the only example in our galaxy. Perhaps civilisations like ours tend to destroy themselves before they can travel or communicate across the stars. Perhaps aliens exist, but are so far away that their signals have not yet reached us. Or perhaps they are communicating in a way we do not yet understand.

Some scientists have proposed the idea of a "Great Filter" — a barrier that most forms of life fail to cross. Perhaps this filter is already behind us, meaning life on Earth is exceptionally rare. Or perhaps the filter lies ahead, which would be a troubling thought indeed.

"If the universe is so vast, and there are so many planets — where are all the aliens?" — Enrico Fermi, 1950.

07 / Form

What Would Aliens Look Like?

If we ever discover intelligent life on another planet, what would it look like? The honest answer is: we simply do not know. Life on Earth developed over billions of years, shaped by specific conditions, random mutations, and evolutionary pressures. On a different planet, with different gravity, atmosphere, temperature, and chemistry, life could take almost any form.

It might not need a head, eyes, or limbs as we understand them. It might perceive the world through senses we cannot imagine. It might not even be based on carbon chemistry — some scientists have speculated about life based on silicon, or life that uses a different liquid instead of water.

However, some scientists believe that evolution tends to produce certain features repeatedly because they are useful — a concept called convergent evolution. Eyes, for example, have evolved independently many times on Earth. It is possible that alien life capable of building technology might share some features with us, at least in a broad sense.

08 / Discovery

What If We Find Aliens?

The discovery of extraterrestrial life — even simple microbes — would be one of the greatest events in human history. It would mean that life is not unique to Earth, and that the universe is far richer and more complex than we ever imagined.

The discovery of intelligent life would be even more profound. It would raise deep questions about our place in the universe, our values, and our identity as a species. How should humanity respond? Should we communicate? What do we say? What if their intentions are unknown?

Some scientists, including the late Stephen Hawking, warned that contact with an advanced alien civilisation could be dangerous — similar to what happened when more technologically advanced civilisations encountered less advanced ones in human history. Others believe that any civilisation capable of reaching us would have already survived the challenges that threaten younger civilisations, suggesting they might be peaceful.

For now, these remain questions without answers. But the search goes on — in our solar system and across the stars — as humanity reaches outward, asking one of the oldest and most profound questions of all: are we alone?
